DATA INTEGRITY GUIDE — ALCOA+ / FDA 2018 / MHRA / AUDIT TRAILS / METADATA
==========================================================================

SECTION 1: ALCOA+ FRAMEWORK
-----------------------------
Data Integrity is governed by the ALCOA+ framework, which defines the attributes
that all GMP data must possess. ALCOA+ is cited by FDA, MHRA, WHO, and EMA.

ALCOA+ stands for:

A — ATTRIBUTABLE
Data must be traceable to the individual who performed the activity or observation.
For electronic data: user ID, timestamp, electronic signature.
For paper data: handwritten signature or initials with date and time.
Shared login credentials are a critical data integrity finding.
System administrators must not share accounts with operators.

L — LEGIBLE
Data must be readable at the time of recording and for the entire retention period.
Pencil entries are not permitted in GMP records.
Corrections must be made with a single strikethrough, initialed, and dated.
Overwriting, use of correction fluid (Tipp-Ex/Whiteout) is prohibited.
Electronic data must remain readable through system changes and migration.

C — CONTEMPORANEOUS
Data must be recorded at the time the activity is performed, not reconstructed later.
Pre-dating, post-dating, or backdating of records is a critical data integrity violation.
Timestamps in electronic systems must be accurate and synchronized (NTP servers).
Batch records must be completed in real time during production.

O — ORIGINAL
Data must be the first capture of the information.
For electronic systems: the raw data file generated by the instrument is original.
Transcription of original data to another medium introduces transcription errors.
If transcription is necessary, second-person verification is required.
Raw data files must be retained; deleting original data files is prohibited.

A — ACCURATE
Data must reflect what was actually observed or measured.
No falsification, manipulation, or fabrication of data.
Rounding rules must be predefined and applied consistently.
Blank entries are not acceptable — use N/A with justification if applicable.

+ (PLUS) COMPLETE
All data including out-of-specification, failed, or repeated analyses must be retained.
Selective reporting of favorable results is data falsification.
Complete audit trail must capture all changes including deleted data.
Laboratory notebooks must account for all pages (no torn-out pages).

+ (PLUS) CONSISTENT
Data entries must be internally consistent with related records.
Timestamps, sequences, and values must be logically consistent.
Cross-reference checks: batch record vs. LIMS vs. equipment logs.

+ (PLUS) ENDURING
Data must be retained for the required retention period in a durable format.
Data stored only in volatile memory without backup is not enduring.
Retention periods: minimum 1 year after expiry date or 3 years after batch disposition.

+ (PLUS) AVAILABLE
Data must be accessible for review and inspection by authorized personnel including regulators.
System privileges must allow read access for QA and auditors.
Archived data must be retrievable within a reasonable timeframe.

SECTION 2: FDA DATA INTEGRITY GUIDANCE 2018
---------------------------------------------
FDA published "Data Integrity and Compliance with Drug CGMP" guidance in December 2018.
Key principles and expectations:

FDA expects CGMP records to be complete, consistent, and accurate.
The "static" raw data (original observations) must be preserved unaltered.

FDA findings from inspections triggering data integrity observations:
- Audit trails disabled or not reviewed during batch record review
- Shared logins for electronic systems
- System clocks not synchronized or deliberately altered
- Data saved in non-raw format only (e.g., only printed PDF retained)
- Data generated and then deleted or overwritten before review
- Test sequences run multiple times with only passing results reported
- "Blank" injections or system suitability failures not documented

FDA remediation expectations after data integrity findings:
1. Retrospective review of data for affected product/period
2. Risk assessment of distributed product
3. Root cause investigation
4. CAPA with systemic corrective measures
5. If fraud is confirmed: referral for criminal investigation

Computer System Validation (CSV) is required to ensure that electronic systems
used to generate, record, or process GMP data comply with 21 CFR Part 11.

SECTION 3: MHRA DATA INTEGRITY GUIDANCE
-----------------------------------------
MHRA published GxP Data Integrity Guidance and Definitions (March 2018).
This is one of the most comprehensive regulatory guides on data integrity.

MHRA defines a Data Governance System as the sum total of arrangements to ensure
data are complete, consistent, and accurate throughout their lifecycle.

Key MHRA expectations:

Data lifecycle management: Data integrity controls must cover the entire lifecycle —
creation, processing, review, reporting, retention, retrieval, and destruction.

Risk-based approach: Organizations must identify data integrity risks and implement
controls proportionate to the risk to product quality and patient safety.

Data integrity culture: Senior management must foster a culture where data integrity
is everyone's responsibility. Staff must feel able to report concerns without fear.

Management responsibilities:
- Adequate training on data integrity principles
- Workload that allows staff sufficient time to complete records correctly
- Systems designed to facilitate data integrity (prompting for mandatory fields, etc.)

Open-access systems: Systems without access controls present inherent data integrity risk.
All systems must have appropriate access controls with individual user accounts.

Self-inspection: Regular self-inspection of data integrity practices, including
review of audit trails, is expected.

SECTION 4: AUDIT TRAIL REQUIREMENTS
--------------------------------------
Audit trails are a fundamental control for electronic GMP data integrity.

Regulatory basis:
- 21 CFR Part 11.10(e): Computer-generated audit trails for GMP systems
- EU GMP Annex 11 §9: Audit trails for systems managing critical data
- MHRA Data Integrity Guidance: Regular audit trail review required
- WHO TRS 996 Annex 5: Computerized systems audit trails

Audit trail must capture at minimum:
- User identification (who)
- Date and time (when) — with system-synchronized timestamp
- Action performed (what): create, modify, delete
- Original value (before change)
- New value (after change)
- Reason for change where required

Audit trail review requirements:
- Must be reviewed as part of batch record review before batch release
- QC laboratory: LIMS and instrument audit trails reviewed per analytical batch
- Review must be documented (reviewer name, date, outcome)
- Anomalies or unexplained entries must be investigated

Audit trail system requirements:
- Must be computer-generated (not user-alterable manual logs)
- Must be protected from modification by any user including administrators
- Must be retained for same period as associated data
- Must be available for regulatory inspection in human-readable format

SECTION 5: METADATA INTEGRITY
-------------------------------
Metadata is data about data — the contextual information needed to understand
the meaning and provenance of the primary data.

Critical metadata examples:
- Instrument identification and calibration status at time of measurement
- Method version used for analysis
- Acquisition parameters (run time, detector settings, injection volume)
- Analyst ID and date/time of analysis
- Sample sequence file with injection order and queue
- Processing method version and integration parameters
- Software version used

Metadata must be retained with the original data. Retaining a PDF report without
the instrument raw data file and acquisition metadata is not compliant.
Raw data for chromatographic systems includes: the chromatogram file, sequence file,
method files, and integration parameters.

SECTION 6: RAW DATA VS PROCESSED DATA
---------------------------------------
Distinction between raw data and processed data is critical for compliance.

RAW DATA (Original Data):
- The first capture of information from an observation or measurement
- Chromatography: the .raw or .dat instrument file with the original signal
- Spectroscopy: the original spectrum file with all wavelengths
- Manual observations: the contemporaneous entry in the paper logbook
- Cannot be altered; any change must create an audit trail

PROCESSED DATA:
- Data derived from raw data by calculation, transformation, or analysis
- Chromatography: integrated area, retention time, concentration calculated
- Reports generated from raw data
- Summary tables in batch records

Both raw and processed data must be retained per GMP requirements.
Processing algorithms must be validated. Changes to processing parameters
that affect the outcome must be justified and documented.

SECTION 7: HYBRID SYSTEMS
---------------------------
Hybrid systems combine electronic data capture with paper-based records.
They present significant data integrity risk if not properly designed and controlled.

Examples of hybrid systems:
- Balance printout stapled to paper logbook entry
- Instrument generates electronic file but data transcribed to paper batch record
- Electronic LIMS with manual paper quality check forms

Hybrid system risks:
- Transcription errors between electronic and paper components
- Inconsistency between electronic record and paper record
- Paper records may not capture all electronic data
- Difficulty in linking paper and electronic records for audit

Controls required for hybrid systems:
- Clear procedures defining which record is the original/master
- Second-person verification of all transcribed data
- Audit of electronic records during paper record review
- Link between electronic file identifier and paper record reference
- Electronic file must be retained even if paper is the controlled record

Phase-out of hybrid systems in favor of fully electronic systems is recommended
to eliminate transcription risks.

SECTION 8: PAPER-BASED SYSTEM GAPS
-------------------------------------
Paper-based systems present inherent data integrity challenges:

Identified risks:
- No automatic timestamp — relies on contemporaneous completion by operator
- No technical access controls — any person with access can alter records
- Correction fluid or pencil use may go undetected
- Pre-printing or pre-signing of records
- "Penciling in" data and inking over later

Controls for paper systems:
- Indelible ink (no pencil permitted)
- Sequential page numbering with accountability for all pages
- Witnessed corrections: single strikethrough, initials, date, reason
- Contemporaneous completion verified by supervisor review
- Secure storage to prevent unauthorized access
- Periodic review by QA for completeness and consistency

Self-inspection checklist items for paper systems:
□ All entries in indelible ink
□ No blank spaces (N/A with signature if not applicable)
□ All corrections made per SOP
□ Records completed contemporaneously (consistent with process flow times)
□ No evidence of pre-signed or pre-dated records
□ Pagination complete with no torn pages
□ Records stored securely with access log

SECTION 9: DATA INTEGRITY PROGRAM IMPLEMENTATION
-------------------------------------------------
Building a sustainable data integrity program requires:

1. Data Criticality Assessment: Map all data flows and classify by GMP criticality.
   Identify which systems generate, process, or store GMP-critical data.

2. System Inventory and Risk Ranking: Rank all systems by data integrity risk.
   Factors: data criticality, access controls, audit trail capability, user base.

3. Procedure Development: SOPs for audit trail review, data backup verification,
   access management, incident reporting, and periodic self-assessment.

4. Training Program: Role-based training on data integrity for all GMP personnel.
   Include case studies from FDA Warning Letters.

5. Self-Inspection Program: Regular audits of data integrity controls.
   Include data walk-throughs and testing of access controls.

6. Metrics and Trending: Track data integrity incidents, CAPA closure rates,
   audit trail anomalies identified, and near-miss reports.

SECTION: HYBRID SYSTEMS DATA INTEGRITY
Hybrid systems combine paper and electronic records and present unique data integrity challenges.
The fundamental risk is that data can be modified after original recording without detection.
FDA expectation: the electronic record is the raw data; paper is a derived copy.

Required controls for hybrid systems per FDA and MHRA guidance:
The electronic record must be secured and cannot be deleted or overwritten by users.
Paper printouts must be generated contemporaneously with the electronic data, not afterward.
Any transcription from electronic to paper must be independently verified by a second qualified person.
The audit trail must capture all print actions, export events, and data transfers to paper.

MHRA Data Integrity Guidance states: "Where hybrid systems are used, the associated risks
should be understood and controlled. The hybrid approach should not be used to circumvent
the requirements of regulations or good data management practices."

SECTION: COMPUTERIZED SYSTEM VALIDATION FOR DATA INTEGRITY
Per FDA Data Integrity Guidance 2018, validated computerized systems must satisfy specific requirements.
Validation alone is insufficient — ongoing controls must be maintained throughout the system lifecycle.

Core technical requirements for GMP computerized systems:
The system must create secure, accurate, and reliable records that cannot be altered without detection.
Audit trail functionality must be enabled and cannot be disabled by routine system users.
The audit trail must record: who made the change, when, what the original value was, what the new value is.
Audit trail must be reviewed as part of batch record review and cannot be bypassed for product release.

User access and identity requirements for data integrity:
User access must be controlled with unique individual accounts — shared logins are never acceptable.
Access levels must be role-based: operator access does not include ability to delete or modify raw data.
The system must prevent backdating of records — timestamps must be system-generated and secured.
System administrator accounts must be controlled, and all administrator actions must be logged.

Data storage and backup requirements:
All data must be stored in a secured, validated location with defined backup procedures.
Backup restoration must be tested periodically to confirm data recovery capability.
Data must be retained for the full retention period defined by regulations and quality procedures.

SECTION: FDA 483 OBSERVATIONS FOR DATA INTEGRITY
FDA inspections routinely identify data integrity deficiencies that result in Warning Letters and Import Alerts.
Understanding the most common observations is essential for preparing systems before inspection.

Most common FDA 483 observations — Category 1: System Configuration
Audit trail not enabled on computerized systems used for GMP activities.
Audit trail review not included in batch record review process before product release.
System clock not secured — users can modify computer date and time to enable backdating.
User accounts with administrative privileges used for routine GMP activities.

Most common FDA 483 observations — Category 2: User Access Controls
Shared user accounts in LIMS, HPLC systems, balances, or other GMP computerized systems.
Generic or group accounts such as "analyst" or "lab" used for GMP data entry.
Terminated employees' accounts not deactivated promptly in GMP systems.
No periodic review of access rights to confirm they remain appropriate for current job functions.

Most common FDA 483 observations — Category 3: Raw Data Management
Electronic raw data deleted or overwritten without proper controls or audit trail entries.
Test injection sequences in HPLC showing sample re-integration without documented justification.
Data generated outside validated systems, such as Excel spreadsheets without access controls.
Chromatographic metadata (injection sequence files, instrument method files) not included in batch documentation.

Remediation expectations when data integrity deficiencies are found:
Immediate CAPA addressing root cause, not just the observed deficiency.
Retrospective assessment of potentially affected batches to determine extent of impact.
System validation updated to include data integrity controls and audit trail requirements.
Training for all GMP personnel on data integrity expectations and regulatory requirements.
Management-level commitment documented with timeline for systemic remediation.

SECTION: ALCOA+ PRINCIPLES IN PRACTICE
ALCOA+ is the framework regulators use to evaluate the quality of GMP records.
Each principle has specific implementation requirements in GMP environments.

ATTRIBUTABLE: Every data entry must be traceable to the person who created it.
Electronic systems: unique user ID with password or biometric authentication.
Paper records: handwritten signature or initials with date. Rubber stamps are not acceptable.
Automated systems: instrument must log the operator ID associated with each result.

LEGIBLE: All records must be readable for the entire retention period.
Handwritten entries: use permanent ink, no pencil, no correction fluid (white-out).
Electronic records: stored in validated formats that remain readable with current software.
Microfilm, microfiche, or electronic archives must be verified readable before paper disposal.

CONTEMPORANEOUS: Records must be made at the time the activity occurs, not reconstructed later.
Paper logs must be filled in while performing the task, not at the end of the shift.
Electronic systems must timestamp entries automatically at the point of data creation.
Pre-recording (filling in data before performing the step) is a critical GMP violation.

ORIGINAL: The first record of an observation or activity is the original — all others are copies.
In HPLC: the instrument raw data file is the original; the printed report is a copy.
Copies must be certified as true copies with: signature, date, and statement of authenticity.
Original records must be retained; destroying originals and keeping only copies is not acceptable.

ACCURATE: Data must reflect the true value without rounding, selection, or manipulation.
Reporting only passing results while discarding failing results is data falsification.
Rounding rules must be defined in SOPs and applied consistently.
Outliers may only be excluded with documented statistical justification and QA approval.

SECTION: DATA INTEGRITY RISK ASSESSMENT METHODOLOGY
A data integrity risk assessment must be conducted for all GMP systems per ISPE GAMP 5 and MHRA guidance.
The assessment identifies where data integrity risks exist and what controls are needed.

Risk assessment scope:
Identify all GMP data flows: where data is created, where it travels, where it is stored, who has access.
Evaluate each system for ALCOA+ compliance — gaps in any principle represent a data integrity risk.
Prioritize systems by criticality: patient safety impact, regulatory submission data, batch release data first.

Common risk areas requiring priority assessment:
Laboratory instruments: HPLC, GC, UV-Vis, dissolution, balance systems — all have standalone computerized systems.
Manufacturing systems: PLC, SCADA, MES, process historian — store critical process parameter data.
Quality systems: LIMS, electronic batch records, deviation management, document management systems.
Paper-based systems: logbooks, paper batch records — risk of post-event documentation or backdating.

Risk control strategy:
For each identified risk, controls must be: preventive (system configuration), detective (audit trail review), or corrective.
Controls must be documented in a Data Integrity Management Plan reviewed and approved by QA.
Residual risk after controls must be formally accepted by management.
The risk assessment must be reviewed annually and updated when systems or processes change.

SECTION: BACKUP AND DISASTER RECOVERY FOR GMP DATA
Backup and disaster recovery procedures are required to protect GMP data from loss per 21 CFR 211.68.
The FDA and MHRA both consider data loss equivalent to falsification in terms of GMP compliance impact.

Backup requirements for GMP systems:
Backup frequency must be commensurate with data criticality: daily minimum for batch release systems.
Backups must be stored in a physically separate location from the primary system.
Backup media must be validated for reliability and tested for restoration on a defined schedule.
Restoration testing must confirm: data completeness, data integrity (checksums), and system functionality.

Disaster recovery planning:
Recovery Time Objective (RTO): maximum acceptable downtime for each GMP system during a disaster.
Recovery Point Objective (RPO): maximum acceptable data loss — how old can restored data be.
Business continuity procedures must exist for continued operations during system unavailability.
Annual disaster recovery drill required for critical GMP systems to verify recovery procedures work.
