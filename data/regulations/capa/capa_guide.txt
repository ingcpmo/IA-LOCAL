CAPA GUIDE — 21 CFR 820.100 / ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS / ISO 13485 / RPN
=======================================================================

SECTION 1: 21 CFR 820.100 CAPA REQUIREMENTS
---------------------------------------------
21 CFR 820.100 establishes CAPA requirements for medical device manufacturers.
The same principles apply broadly to pharmaceutical GMP under 21 CFR 211.
FDA considers CAPA one of the most critical quality system elements.

21 CFR 820.100(a) requires procedures for:
(1) Analyzing processes, work operations, concessions, quality audit reports,
    quality records, service records, complaints, returned product, and other
    sources of quality data to identify existing and potential causes of
    nonconforming product or other quality problems.
(2) Investigating the cause of nonconformities relating to product, processes,
    and quality system.
(3) Identifying the action needed to correct and prevent recurrence of
    nonconforming product and other quality problems.
(4) Verifying or validating the corrective and preventive action to ensure
    that such action is effective and does not adversely affect finished device.
(5) Implementing and recording changes in methods and procedures needed to
    correct and prevent identified quality problems.
(6) Ensuring that information related to quality problems or nonconforming
    product is disseminated to those directly responsible for assuring the
    quality of such product or the prevention of such problems.
(7) Submitting relevant information on identified quality problems, as well
    as corrective and preventive actions, for management review.

21 CFR 820.100(b) requires documentation of all CAPA activities and results.
CAPA records must be maintained per 21 CFR 820.180 for at least 2 years from
the date of release or life of the device, whichever is longer.

SECTION 2: ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS — 5-WHY METHOD
-----------------------------------------------
The 5-Why method is a systematic approach to root cause analysis developed
by Toyota and widely applied in GMP quality systems.

Process:
1. Define the problem statement clearly and factually
2. Ask "Why did this happen?" — document the immediate cause
3. Ask "Why did that happen?" — document the contributing cause
4. Continue asking "Why?" until the fundamental systemic root cause is identified
5. Typically 3-7 iterations required; stop when the cause can be addressed by action

Example in GMP context:
Problem: OOS result for API assay in batch 2026-001

Why 1: Analyst prepared standard solution incorrectly
Why 2: Analyst used incorrect solvent (methanol instead of acetonitrile)
Why 3: Solvent bottles were stored adjacent and labels were damaged
Why 4: No solvent labeling SOP specifying label inspection before use
Why 5: Risk assessment for analytical procedure did not identify solvent selection
       as a failure mode requiring procedural control

Root Cause: Inadequate risk assessment for analytical procedure leading to
missing procedural control for critical solvent selection step.

CAPA: Update analytical procedure risk assessment; revise SOP to require
label inspection and second-person verification for critical reagents.

5-Why limitations:
- Different investigators may arrive at different root causes
- Complex problems may have multiple root causes
- Linear approach may miss systemic causes
- Best applied in combination with other RCA methods

SECTION 3: ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS — FISHBONE / ISHIKAWA DIAGRAM
--------------------------------------------------------------
The Fishbone (Ishikawa or Cause-and-Effect) diagram provides a structured
visual method for identifying potential root causes across multiple categories.

Standard categories (6M framework):
- Man (People): Training, competency, human error, fatigue, communication
- Machine (Equipment): Calibration, maintenance, age, software, setup
- Method (Process): SOP inadequacy, procedure not followed, undefined process
- Material: Raw material quality, supplier, storage, labeling
- Measurement: Instrument accuracy, sampling method, measurement frequency
- Mother Nature (Environment): Temperature, humidity, contamination, vibration

Construction process:
1. Define the problem (effect) and place at the head of the "fish"
2. Draw main bones for each of the 6M categories
3. Brainstorm potential causes in each category with subject matter experts
4. Add sub-causes branching from each main cause
5. Evaluate all identified causes and prioritize for investigation
6. Verify or disprove each prioritized cause with evidence

Fishbone advantages:
- Visual and comprehensive — less likely to miss categories of causes
- Team-based approach captures diverse expertise
- Good documentation tool for CAPA record
- Identifies both immediate and systemic causes

For GMP investigations, the fishbone should be completed with cross-functional
team including production, QC, QA, engineering, and/or validation as appropriate.

SECTION 4: ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS — FAULT TREE ANALYSIS (FTA)
------------------------------------------------------------
Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) is a top-down deductive analytical method that models
the pathways through which a system can fail.

FTA uses Boolean logic gates:
- AND gate: All inputs must occur for output to occur
- OR gate: Any one input can cause output to occur
- Basic Event: Undeveloped fault event (component failure)
- Undeveloped Event: Fault event not further analyzed
- Inhibit Gate: Output occurs only when input and condition both occur

FTA process:
1. Define the top-level undesired event (top event)
2. Identify all direct causes — connected by AND/OR gates
3. Continue expanding each cause until basic events are reached
4. Calculate probability of top event if failure probabilities known
5. Identify minimum cut sets (minimal combination of basic events that cause top event)
6. Prioritize corrective actions targeting minimum cut sets with highest probability

FTA is particularly valuable for:
- Critical system failures with potential safety impact
- Complex multi-component failure scenarios
- Identifying single points of failure requiring redundancy
- Demonstrating that combined probability of failure meets safety targets

FTA is required for process hazard analysis in pharmaceutical manufacturing
when conducting risk assessments for complex automated systems.

SECTION 5: EFFECTIVENESS CRITERIA FOR CAPA
--------------------------------------------
CAPA effectiveness verification is required to confirm that the corrective action
resolved the root cause and that the problem did not recur.

Types of effectiveness criteria:

Quantitative Metrics:
- Zero recurrence of same nonconformity classification within defined period
- Reduction in OOS rate for specific analytical method below defined threshold
- Reduction in deviation rate for specific process step
- Audit trail review shows no repeat of identified data integrity violation
- Employee competency assessment score above defined threshold

Qualitative Criteria:
- Updated SOP reviewed and approved
- Training completed for all affected personnel with verified comprehension
- Revised procedure followed correctly in subsequent batches (verified by supervision)
- Control measure implemented and functioning as designed

Timeframe for effectiveness verification:
- Minimum observation period should be sufficient to allow recurrence if not fixed
- Typically 3-6 months or 3-5 production batches, whichever is longer
- High-risk CAPAs may require extended observation period

Effectiveness check failure:
- If effectiveness check fails, CAPA must be re-opened
- Root cause analysis must be reviewed and expanded
- Additional corrective actions implemented
- FDA expectation: systematic approach escalated if initial CAPA fails

Documentation of effectiveness:
- Effectiveness verification plan defined at CAPA opening
- Criteria, measurement method, responsible person, and due date documented
- Effectiveness check results documented with objective evidence
- QA approval of effectiveness determination before CAPA closure

SECTION 6: CAPA CLOSURE REQUIREMENTS
---------------------------------------
CAPA closure is the formal determination that all required actions are complete
and effective. Premature closure is a common FDA audit finding.

Pre-closure checklist:
□ Root cause identified and documented with supporting evidence
□ All corrective actions implemented with objective evidence
□ All preventive actions implemented
□ Changed documents reviewed, approved, and in effect
□ Training completed for all affected personnel with records
□ Effectiveness verification completed per defined criteria
□ Effectiveness criteria met with objective evidence
□ Related CAPAs/deviations reviewed for similar root causes
□ QA review and approval of closure package

CAPA closure documentation package must include:
- CAPA description and source reference (deviation, OOS, audit finding)
- Root cause analysis with methodology and conclusion
- Corrective action description, responsible person, and completion date
- Preventive action description and implementation evidence
- Training records or notification evidence
- Effectiveness verification plan and results
- QA approval signature and date

Open CAPA management:
- CAPAs open beyond 90 days require documented justification and management approval
- CAPAs open beyond 180 days require escalation to site management review
- Overdue CAPAs are a key quality metric for management review

SECTION 7: RECURRING DEVIATION PREVENTION
-------------------------------------------
Recurring deviations indicate that previous corrective actions were ineffective
or that the root cause was not correctly identified.

Identification of recurring deviations:
- Same description or category recurring within defined period (typically 12 months)
- Same equipment, process step, personnel, or material involved
- Pattern identified through deviation trending analysis (monthly/quarterly)

Regulatory expectations for recurring deviations:
- 21 CFR 211.192 requires investigation of discrepancies with conclusions
- FDA Warning Letters cite "failure to adequately investigate recurring deviations"
- EU GMP requires trend analysis to identify potential systemic issues

Investigation approach for recurring deviations:
1. Confirm recurrence: verify same or similar root cause, not coincidental similarity
2. Review previous CAPA: was it fully implemented? Was effectiveness verified?
3. Expand root cause analysis: initial analysis may have been too narrow
4. Systemic assessment: is the problem isolated or representative of broader failure?
5. Escalated CAPA: stronger corrective measures, process redesign if needed

Common systemic causes for recurring deviations:
- Training: procedure understood but not retained; need for refresher or retraining
- Procedure design: procedure difficult to follow correctly; need redesign
- Equipment reliability: underlying equipment issue not resolved
- Organizational: workload or resource constraints driving shortcuts
- Culture: lack of appreciation for importance of compliance

SECTION 8: FDA EXPECTATIONS FOR CAPA DOCUMENTATION
-----------------------------------------------------
FDA Warning Letters consistently cite inadequate CAPA documentation.
Key FDA expectations:

Problem Description:
- Clear, specific, unambiguous description of the nonconformance
- Reference to applicable regulation, specification, or SOP
- Scope determination: is this isolated or does it affect other products/processes?

Root Cause Investigation:
- Documented methodology (5-Why, fishbone, FTA)
- Evidence supporting root cause conclusion
- Evidence ruling out other potential causes
- "Unknown" is not an acceptable root cause conclusion without exhaustive investigation

CAPA Implementation:
- Specific actions, not vague commitments ("will retrain staff")
- Named responsible persons and specific due dates
- Objective evidence of completion (training records, revised SOPs with dates)

Effectiveness:
- Predetermined criteria, not post-hoc determination
- Objective measures, not subjective assessment
- Adequate observation period

Systemic Assessment:
- Was the same nonconformance identified in other products, processes, or areas?
- If yes: scope CAPA to all affected areas
- If no: documented justification for limited scope

FDA inspection preparation for CAPA:
- Maintain CAPA log accessible for inspection review
- Be able to walk through a CAPA end-to-end with documentation
- Demonstrate that effectiveness checks were completed with evidence

SECTION 9: ISO 13485 CAPA REQUIREMENTS
----------------------------------------
ISO 13485:2016 (Section 8.5.2 and 8.5.3) defines CAPA requirements for
medical device quality management systems.

Corrective Action (8.5.2):
The organization shall document a procedure to define requirements for:
a) Reviewing nonconformities (including complaints)
b) Determining the causes of nonconformities
c) Evaluating the need for action to ensure nonconformities do not recur
d) Planning and implementing needed action, including updating documentation
e) Verifying that corrective actions do not adversely affect the ability to meet
   applicable regulatory requirements or the safety and performance of the device
f) Reviewing the effectiveness of corrective action taken

Preventive Action (8.5.3):
The organization shall document a procedure to define requirements for:
a) Determining potential nonconformities and their causes
b) Evaluating the need for action to prevent occurrence of nonconformities
c) Determining and implementing action needed
d) Recording results of investigations and actions taken
e) Reviewing the effectiveness of preventive action taken

ISO 13485 differs from ISO 9001 in that it maintains separate requirements
for corrective and preventive action, reflecting the medical device industry's
need for explicit preventive quality management.

Risk-based CAPA prioritization per ISO 14971 (Risk Management for Medical Devices):
- High risk (high severity × high probability): immediate escalation, mandatory CAPA
- Medium risk: CAPA required within defined timeframe
- Low risk: may be addressed through process improvement rather than formal CAPA

SECTION 10: RPN-BASED CAPA PRIORITIZATION
------------------------------------------
Risk Priority Number (RPN) is a quantitative risk assessment tool from FMEA
(Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) used to prioritize CAPA actions.

RPN = Severity (S) × Occurrence (O) × Detectability (D)

Severity scale (1-10):
1-3: Minor — no effect on safety or efficacy
4-6: Moderate — potential impact on product quality
7-9: Significant — potential safety or regulatory impact
10: Critical — direct patient safety risk

Occurrence scale (1-10):
1-2: Remote — unlikely to occur (< 1 in 10,000)
3-4: Low — occasional occurrence (1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000)
5-6: Moderate — occasional occurrence (1 in 100 to 1 in 1,000)
7-8: High — repeated occurrence (1 in 10 to 1 in 100)
9-10: Very High — failure almost inevitable (> 1 in 10)

Detectability scale (1-10):
1-2: Almost certain detection — controls will almost certainly detect
3-4: High detection — controls likely to detect before reaching patient
5-6: Moderate — controls may detect
7-8: Low — controls unlikely to detect
9-10: Almost impossible — no controls capable of detection

CAPA prioritization by RPN:
- RPN > 200: Critical — immediate CAPA mandatory, escalate to management
- RPN 100-200: High — CAPA required within 30 days
- RPN 50-100: Medium — CAPA required within 90 days
- RPN < 50: Low — monitor and address as part of continuous improvement

Verification vs. Validation of CAPA Effectiveness:

Verification of Effectiveness:
- Confirms that the specific action was completed as described
- Example: verify that training was completed (check training records)
- Example: verify that SOP was revised (check document management system)
- Does not confirm that the action actually solved the problem

Validation of Effectiveness:
- Confirms that the action actually resolved the root cause
- Requires observation period and measurement against predefined criteria
- Example: zero recurrence of same deviation in next 6 months / 10 batches
- Example: OOS rate for specific method below defined threshold
- This is what FDA inspectors look for when auditing CAPA effectiveness

Both verification AND validation of effectiveness are required for complete CAPA closure.
Verification confirms implementation; validation confirms impact on quality.

SECTION: CAPA TRENDING AND SYSTEMIC ANALYSIS
Per ICH Q10 and 21 CFR 820.100, CAPA data must be analyzed for trends to identify systemic issues.
Trending must be proactive, not reactive — the goal is to detect patterns before they cause product failures.

Monthly trending requirements for CAPA programs:
All open CAPAs categorized by severity (Critical, Major, Minor), area (production, QC, QA, warehouse),
and root cause category (human error, equipment, process, documentation, supplier, environment).
Overdue CAPAs must be escalated to department management with updated action plan within 5 business days.
New CAPAs opened in the period compared to prior periods to identify increasing failure rates by area.

Quarterly management review of CAPA trends:
Recurring root causes across different products, equipment, or departments must be identified and escalated.
Cross-functional review required when same root cause appears in more than two CAPAs within 90 days.
CAPA backlog analysis: confirm resources are adequate and timelines are realistic and being met.

Annual effectiveness analysis of the CAPA program:
Percentage of CAPAs with verified effectiveness versus total CAPAs closed in the year.
Recurrence analysis: how many CAPAs resulted in the same or similar issue recurring within 12 months.
Time-to-close metrics by CAPA category to identify systemic delays in the quality system.

Systemic indicators requiring immediate escalation to senior management:
Same root cause identified in more than two CAPAs within 90 days from any area.
CAPA effectiveness failure where the same issue recurs within 1 year of CAPA closure.
CAPA open for more than 120 days without documented progress update or extension approval.
FDA or regulatory agency finding with a CAPA commitment not met by the committed date.

SECTION: FDA EXPECTATIONS FOR CAPA DOCUMENTATION
FDA inspectors evaluate CAPA programs against specific criteria during GMP inspections.
A well-documented CAPA program is one of the strongest indicators of a mature quality system.

Timeliness expectations by CAPA severity:
Critical CAPAs must have an action plan within 30 days of opening.
Major CAPAs must have an action plan within 60 days of opening.
Minor CAPAs must have an action plan within 90 days of opening.
Any CAPA extending beyond these timelines must have documented justification and QA approval.

Root cause analysis quality requirements:
CAPA must address the true root cause, not the symptom or the immediate cause.
Acceptable root cause methods: 5-Why analysis, Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, Fault Tree Analysis.
Root cause conclusion must be supported by evidence — not just "analyst error" without investigation.
Extent of condition assessment must evaluate whether the root cause could affect other areas or products.

Scope and systemic considerations:
Every CAPA must consider whether the issue could occur in similar processes, equipment, or products.
Scope of the CAPA action must be explicitly documented — "this product only" requires justification.
When scope is expanded, affected products and batches must be evaluated for impact.

Effectiveness verification requirements:
Verification criteria must be defined before CAPA implementation, not selected after the fact.
Criteria must be objective and measurable, for example: zero recurrence in the next 6 months,
or OOS rate for the affected method below a defined threshold for the next 10 batches.
Observation period must be sufficient to confirm effectiveness — typically 3 to 12 months depending on frequency.

CAPA closure requirements per FDA expectations:
Evidence of effectiveness must be objective data, not a statement that "the CAPA was effective."
QA must review and sign off the effectiveness evidence before formal CAPA closure.
Recurrence detection mechanism must be confirmed active before closure.

Common FDA 483 observations for CAPA programs:
"Failure to establish and maintain procedures for implementing corrective and preventive action" — 21 CFR 820.100.
"CAPA system does not include requirements for verifying or validating corrective and preventive actions."
"Trends in product failures and quality data not evaluated to identify recurring problems requiring systemic CAPA."
"CAPAs closed without objective evidence that the corrective action was effective."
"Extent of condition assessment not performed — CAPA limited to affected batch without evaluation of other products."
